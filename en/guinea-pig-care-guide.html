Guinea Pig Care Guide

Guinea pig care: fiber-rich diet, vitamin C, safe flooring, water and warning signs.

Species-specific careNutrition and environmentSources & limitations

Before using this guide

This page is a starting point for responsible care, not a veterinary diagnosis. Needs vary with species, age and health. Verify the exact species and speak to an appropriately experienced veterinarian.

🌍 Native range, habitat & climate

Native range / origins

Domesticated guinea-pig lineage from Andean South America

What wild habitat means for care

A large solid-floored habitat, compatible social company, hay and vitamin C are important.

Climate and temperature

Merck housing reference is 18–26°C with 30–60% relative humidity; monitor overheating and airflow.

Outdoor climate is not a thermostat setpoint. Each species needs its own husbandry plan.

View origin / climate source ↗

Fiber-rich diet

Suitable grass hay is a dietary foundation for adult guinea pigs, with species-specific pellets and safe vegetables. Change diets gradually.

Vitamin C requirement

Guinea pigs cannot synthesize enough vitamin C. Consult a veterinarian about fresh food sources and supplementation when indicated.

Housing and water

Provide a roomy, ventilated enclosure with solid flooring, hiding areas and clean water. Check bottle nozzles and hygiene daily.

Urgent warning signs

Seek prompt exotic-veterinary care for lack of appetite, fewer droppings, marked weakness or breathing problems.

Daily care and early-warning checklist

  • Keep suitable hay and clean water available; discuss a species-appropriate vitamin C source with an experienced veterinarian.
  • Offer compatible social companionship, hiding areas and clean, dry footing.
  • Monitor appetite, droppings, weight trend and chewing or dental changes.
  • Seek prompt small-mammal veterinary care for refusal to eat, weakness or breathing difficulty.

This checklist is not a substitute for an individual examination. Dated notes on significant changes can help at a veterinary visit.

EVIDENCE-INFORMED CARE

Feeding, unsafe foods & common health concerns

Guidance varies by exact species, age and medical conditions. A food a pet likes is not necessarily safe or nutritionally balanced.

Suitable foods

Provide continuous quality grass hay, guinea-pig-specific vitamin C-fortified pellets and clean suitable greens. Bell pepper is an example of a vitamin C-containing vegetable.

Foods and practices to avoid

Rabbit pellets may not meet guinea pigs’ vitamin C needs. Avoid frequent sugary treats, spoiled feed and abrupt diet changes.

Common health problems

Vitamin C deficiency, dental disorders, respiratory disease and skin problems can occur. Track weight, chewing and enclosure hygiene.

Urgent signs

Refusal to eat, fewer droppings, drooling, breathing difficulty or marked lethargy need prompt exotic-mammal veterinary attention. Do not give human medicines or arbitrary antibiotics.

What this guide does not do

Lifespans, healthy weights, vaccination needs, safe foods and optimal temperatures cannot be generalized to every animal. This page does not prescribe drugs, vaccines or treatment. Seek prompt veterinary help for acute symptoms.
